Vertex form: f of x equals a times x minus h, squared, plus k. The vertex is h comma k. That's it — you read it straight from the equation.

One catch. Look at the form: x minus h. The h is subtracted. So the sign you see is the opposite of h.

If you see x minus 4, then h equals positive 4.
If you see x plus 3 — which is the same as x minus negative 3 — then h equals negative 3.

The sign flips. This is the most common mistake with vertex form, and the SAT uses it on purpose. k has no trick: whatever's at the end, that's your vertex y-value.

A few quick examples to lock this in.

f of x equals 3 times x minus 4 squared plus 7. Vertex: 4 comma 7. a is positive 3, opens up, minimum. Minimum value: 7. Axis: x equals 4.

f of x equals negative 2 times x plus 5 squared minus 1. x plus 5 means h is negative 5. k is negative 1. Vertex: negative 5 comma negative 1. a negative, opens down, maximum. Maximum value: negative 1. Axis: x equals negative 5.

f of x equals x minus 1 squared. No written coefficient, so a is 1. No constant at the end, so k is 0. Vertex: 1 comma 0. Vertex sits right on the x-axis, which means there's exactly one root, at x equals 1.

The axis of symmetry in vertex form is just x equals h. Same x-coordinate as the vertex.

Converting from vertex form back to standard form: expand the binomial, multiply through by a, collect terms. Mechanical.

Going the other direction — standard to vertex — requires completing the square, which you just learned.

On the SAT, vertex form problems almost always ask you to read information off the equation. Given the form, identify the vertex, axis, or min/max. The speed is the point. Once you've practiced enough that the sign-flip on h is reflex, these questions take under thirty seconds.
